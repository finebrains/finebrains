The green option

The computer is the pool heater.

One liquid-cooled AI compute node, plumbed into a pool or spa like any other heater. Nearly every watt it draws leaves as warm pool water — instead of hot air thrown away.

No separate gas heater to run. No extra heat pump for holding temperature. The heat is a by-product of work the computer was already doing.

How it works

Four parts. Simple on purpose.

AI node → Sealed coolant loop → Titanium exchanger → Pool / spa

1 — HEAT SOURCE

The node makes the heat

A liquid-cooled 8-GPU node with cold plates on every chip. Essentially all its electrical draw becomes heat in the coolant.

2 — INSIDE THE BOX

Coolant moves it

A pump and manifold (the CDU) circulate the sealed coolant from the chips to the exchanger — and back, at about 35–45 °C.

3 — THE WALL

Titanium exchanger

Pool water is chlorinated, so only titanium survives it. The two loops touch metal here — they never mix.

4 — SAFETY VALVE

Dry cooler fallback

When the pool is warm enough or its pump stops, a valve sends heat to an outdoor radiator instead, so the compute keeps running.

Why this is the green option

One electricity bill does two jobs: compute, and heating.

♻️ Heat already paid for

At 15 kW running around the clock, the node makes about 131,000 kWh of heat a year. Bought separately, that heat would cost roughly $4,700/yr from a heat pump or $7,200/yr from gas. Here it costs nothing extra.

🔥 No gas burned

No gas line, no refrigerant, no compressor. Warm coolant at 35–45 °C heats a 26–30 °C pool directly.

❄️ Free cooling, too

The pool is a large, patient heat sink. The compute owner gets cooling, and the pool owner gets heat — both want the node running.

The same idea is already proven at this scale: UK startup Deep Green runs a ~28 kW compute cluster heating a public swimming pool, turning about 96% of its electricity into pool heat and cutting the pool's gas use by about 62%.

What it does well — honestly

This is a holding and steady-warming heater, not a fast heat-up heater.

Holds a covered pool

About 0.32 °F per hour for a typical 19,200-gallon inground pool — about 7.7 °F per day of full-load running. Enough to hold temperature and build it slowly.

Heats a spa fast

About 12.3 °F per hour for a 500-gallon spa, so the spa reaches temperature quickly, then the system returns to pool duty.

It will not fast-heat a cold inground pool like a 100,000–140,000 BTU/hr heater. That honesty is part of the offer: steady, free heat, around the clock — covered pool or not, sun or not.
